# Voyager 2 at Uranus

After completing its primary encounters with Jupiter and Saturn, Voyager 2 continues to Uranus, a journey of about 4,5 years. Its Jupiter encounter is optimized in part to make subsequent planetary flybys possible. The geometry of its Uranus encounter also preserves the possibility of a later flyby of Neptune. Voyager 1 had only 6.4 days of close study during its flyby.

Voyager 2 is the first human-made object to fly past Uranus. Its short-range observations begin before closest approach, and signals take approximately 2,5 hours to reach Earth. Sunlight at Uranus is approximately 400 times weaker than at Earth. Closest approach occurs at 17:59 UT Jan. 24, 1986, at a range of about 81,500 kilometers (50,640 miles).

During the flyby, Voyager 2 discovers 10 new moons—Puck, Portia, Juliet, Cressida, Rosalind, Belinda, Desdemona, Cordelia, Ophelia, and Bianca—whose names draw on characters from Shakespeare and Alexander Pope. It also discovers two new rings, bringing the known total to 11, and finds a magnetic field tilted at 59 degrees to the rotation axis and offset from the planet’s center. The spacecraft measures atmospheric winds as high as 450 km/h. Its observations also inform models of Uranus’s deep, water-bearing interior. Its rings were found to be extremely variable in thickness and transparency.

Voyager 2 returns images of Miranda, Oberon, Ariel, Umbriel, and Titania, five of Uranus’s moons. At a range of only 17,560 miles (28,260 kilometers), its flyby of Miranda brings the spacecraft closer to an object than any earlier encounter on its journey. Miranda’s images show a striking patchwork of seemingly unrelated surface features, while Uranus itself appears generally featureless.

News of the Uranus encounter is overshadowed by the Challenger accident on Jan. 28, 1986, which kills seven astronauts during their space shuttle launch.
