# Voyager 2 at Uranus

Voyager 2, the first human-made object to fly past Uranus, made its closest approach at 17:59 UT on Jan. 24, 1986, at a range of about 81,500 kilometers (50,640 miles). Radio signals took approximately 2,5 hours to reach Earth. Sunlight at Uranus was about 1/400 as intense as at Earth.

In fact, its encounter with Jupiter was optimized in part to ensure that future planetary flybys would be possible. After Voyager 2 completed its primary mission goals at Jupiter and Saturn, mission planners directed the spacecraft to Uranus—a journey of about 4,5 years. The Uranus encounter geometry also allowed a later encounter with Neptune. Voyager 1 had only 6.4 days of close study during its Saturn flyby.

During its flyby, Voyager 2 discovered 10 new moons: Puck, Portia, Juliet, Cressida, Rosalind, Belinda, Desdemona, Cordelia, Ophelia, and Bianca. It discovered two new rings, bringing Uranus’ known total to 11, and found a magnetic field tilted at 59 degrees off-axis and off-center. The spacecraft found wind speeds in Uranus’ atmosphere as high as 450 km/h (450000 meters per hour). Voyager 2 did not find a boiling lake of water beneath the cloud tops. Its rings were found to be extremely variable in thickness and transparency.

Voyager 2 also returned spectacular photos of Miranda, Oberon, Ariel, and Umbriel, four of Uranus’ smaller moons. Titan is a moon of Saturn, not Uranus. The Miranda flyby was Voyager 2’s closest approach to any object in its nearly decade-long travels, at a range of only 17.560 miles (28.260 kilometers). Images of the moon showed a strange object whose surface was a mishmash of peculiar features that seemed to have no rhyme or reason. Uranus itself appeared generally featureless.

News coverage of the Uranus encounter was interrupted by the Challenger disaster, which occurred on Jan. 28, 1986, four days after Voyager 2’s closest approach, and killed seven astronauts during the space shuttle launch.
