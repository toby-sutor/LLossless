# Voyager 2 at Uranus

After Voyager 2 completed its primary mission goals at Jupiter and Saturn, the flight from Saturn to Uranus took about 4,5 years. Its encounter with Jupiter was optimized in part to ensure that future planetary flybys would be possible. Voyager 2’s Uranus trajectory also enabled its later encounter with Neptune. Voyager 1 did not fly past Uranus; it had only 6.4 days of close study during its Saturn flyby.

Voyager 2 was the first human-made object to fly past Uranus. Its signals took approximately 2,5 hours to reach Earth. Closest approach took place at 17:59 UT on Jan. 24, 1986, at a range of about 81,500 kilometers (50,640 miles). Sunlight at Uranus is about 1/400 as intense as on Earth.

During its flyby, Voyager 2 discovered 10 new moons—Cordelia, Ophelia, Bianca, Cressida, Desdemona, Juliet, Portia, Rosalind, Belinda, and Puck—and two new rings. The moon names follow a literary naming tradition drawn from Shakespeare and Alexander Pope. Uranus’s magnetic field is tilted about 59 degrees from its rotational axis and is offset from the planet’s center. Wind speeds in Uranus’s atmosphere reach about 900 km/h; Voyager 2 found no evidence of a boiling lake of water beneath the clouds. The rings vary greatly in thickness and transparency.

Voyager 2 returned images of Miranda, Oberon, Ariel, Umbriel, and Titania, five of Uranus’s smaller moons. It passed Miranda at about 29,000 kilometers (18,000 miles), its closest approach to any object in its travels to that point. Images showed a surface marked by a seemingly irregular mix of unusual features. Uranus itself appeared generally featureless.

Four days after the Uranus encounter, the Challenger disaster killed seven astronauts during the space shuttle launch on Jan. 28, 1986.
