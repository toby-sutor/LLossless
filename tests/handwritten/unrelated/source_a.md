Japan: The One Trip That Ruins All Your Other Trips

Let me be honest with you: after Japan, everywhere else feels slightly broken.

You'll stand on a platform in Tokyo, watch a bullet train arrive to the second, and something in your brain will quietly recalibrate. The Shinkansen's average annual delay is 36 seconds. Not minutes. Seconds. And that obsessive care isn't reserved for trains — it's in the ¥1,000 bowl of ramen served with the attentiveness of a Michelin dining room, in the convenience store that's cleaner than most supermarkets back home, in the taxi driver's white gloves.

Five reasons to stop hesitating

1. You can exhale. Japan is consistently among the safest countries on Earth. People leave laptops on café tables to hold their seat. Children ride the subway alone. You will spend two weeks without once doing the instinctive pocket-check for your wallet — and you'll only notice the relief when you get home and it comes back.

2. It's cheaper than you think. This is the part that surprises everyone. With the yen where it's been since late 2022, you're effectively getting a substantial discount on everything, plus a 10% tax refund on shopping as a non-resident. A comfortable day — good hotel, excellent meals, a day trip — runs roughly ¥25,000–45,000 per person. Budget travellers manage on ¥7,000–12,000. That's Tokyo. Compare it to London or New York and tell me Japan is expensive.

3. The density is absurd. Tokyo to Osaka is 2.5 hours by train, and inside that corridor sits more UNESCO heritage, world-class food, and genuine strangeness than most entire countries offer. Tokyo alone could occupy you for 100 days. You are never travelling far to find the next extraordinary thing.

4. The food has no bad tier. Not "good restaurants exist" — there is no floor. A ¥900 lunch from a vending-machine ticket counter in a train station will be one of your best meals of the year. Sushi in Tokyo, okonomiyaki in Osaka, kaiseki in Kyoto, wagyu that dissolves.

5. The fears are unfounded. Language barrier? Translation apps plus widespread English signage in tourist areas plus a culture of genuinely going out of their way to help you. Getting lost on the trains? Tap a Suica card and follow the colour-coded signs. Picky eater? You'll be fine.

What your first week actually looks like

- Days 1–3 — Tokyo. Shibuya's crossing, Shinjuku's neon canyons, Asakusa's old-Tokyo temple streets.
- Day 4 — Shinkansen to Kyoto. 2h15m. Watch for Fuji out the right-hand window.
- Days 4–6 — Kyoto. Fushimi Inari's thousand vermilion gates at dawn, Arashiyama's bamboo grove, Gion's wooden lantern-lit lanes. Day-trip to Nara to be politely mugged by deer.
- Day 7 — Osaka. Dotonbori, Kuromon Market, and a flight home from Kansai.

Ten to fourteen days lets you add Hakone's onsen with Fuji views, or Hiroshima and the floating shrine at Miyajima.

When to go

Late March to mid-April for cherry blossom; November for autumn maples. Both are spectacular, both are busy, both are worth it. But here's the secret: Japan has no bad season. Okinawa's beaches, Hokkaido's powder skiing, summer festivals, winter snow monkeys steaming in mountain hot springs.

One practical note

Most nationalities get 90 days visa-free — but check your requirements at least three months out, and book accommodation early for blossom and foliage season. The good ryokan go fast.
