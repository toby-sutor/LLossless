China: The Country That's Too Big to Summarise — So Let's Try Anyway

Most people think they know what a trip to China looks like. Great Wall, Forbidden City, maybe a panda. Then they go, and discover they've essentially visited one room of a mansion with forty of them.

Here's the scale problem, stated plainly: China spans five geographic time zones, holds 60 UNESCO World Heritage sites — second most on Earth — and contains landscapes ranging from tropical rice terraces to Gobi desert to Himalayan plateau. You cannot "do" China. You can only choose your China, and come back for the rest.

Five reasons to book now

1. The travel infrastructure is genuinely the best on the planet. China has over 45,000 km of high-speed rail — more than the rest of the world combined. Beijing to Shanghai, 1,200 km, in 4.5 hours, on a train that hits 350 km/h and has a socket at every seat. Cities you've never heard of have airports and metro systems that would be the pride of a European capital. Getting around is not the hard part. It's embarrassingly easy.

2. Visa-free entry has quietly transformed everything. This is the news most travellers haven't caught up with. China now offers visa-free entry of up to 30 days to citizens of dozens of countries — much of Europe, Australia, New Zealand, Japan, Korea, Malaysia and more — plus a 240-hour (10-day) visa-free transit policy covering 55 nationalities, including the US, UK and Canada, through 60+ ports of entry. The single biggest barrier to visiting China has largely been removed. Check your own nationality's current status, because it keeps expanding in travellers' favour.

3. The value is extraordinary. A high-speed rail seat that would cost €200 in Europe costs €60. A superb regional meal runs ¥40–80. Four-star city hotels sit comfortably at ¥400–700 a night. You can travel well in China for a fraction of what comparable quality costs in Japan or Western Europe — and exceptionally well for what mid-range costs elsewhere.

4. The food isn't a cuisine, it's eight of them. "Chinese food" is a category error. Sichuan's numbing peppercorn heat, Cantonese dim sum at its dawn-service source, Xi'an's hand-pulled biang biang noodles and cumin lamb, Shanghai's soup dumplings, Yunnan's mushrooms and mint, Xinjiang's charcoal skewers and naan. Regional pride is fierce and delicious. Eating your way across China is a legitimate itinerary in itself.

5. The sheer, jaw-loosening scale of the sights. The Terracotta Army — 8,000 individually-faced warriors, still being excavated. The karst pinnacles of Guilin rising out of river mist. Zhangjiajie's sandstone columns that inspired Avatar. The Longji rice terraces. The Mogao Caves' 1,600 years of Buddhist art in the desert. Shanghai's Bund facing a skyline that didn't exist in 1990.

What a first trip actually looks like

The Classic Triangle (10–12 days) — the best introduction there is:

- Beijing, days 1–4. The Forbidden City, the Temple of Heaven, hutong alleys, and a day at a quiet section of the Great Wall — Mutianyu or Jinshanling, not Badaling.
- Xi'an, days 5–7. Overnight or 4.5h by rail. Terracotta Army, the intact Ming city walls you can cycle around, and the Muslim Quarter's food street after dark.
- Shanghai, days 8–11. Fast train across the country. Colonial Bund, Yu Garden, the world's best skyline view, and a day trip to the canal towns of Suzhou or Zhujiajiao.

Add if you have two weeks: Guilin and Yangshuo for the river karsts, Chengdu for pandas and Sichuan hotpot, or Hong Kong via the high-speed line.

The practical bit nobody warns you about

Two things will make or break your trip, and both are easily solved:

- Set up mobile payment before you go. Alipay and WeChat Pay now accept foreign credit cards directly. China is functionally cashless — this is the single most important piece of prep.
- Sort your connectivity. An international roaming eSIM, or a VPN installed before arrival, keeps your usual apps working.

Get those two right and the rest is smooth. Get them wrong and you'll spend day one frustrated. This is exactly the kind of thing we handle for you.

When to go

April–May and September–October are the sweet spots: warm, clear, comfortable. Avoid the first week of October and Chinese New Year, when 1.4 billion people travel simultaneously. Winter is cold in the north but crowd-free at the Great Wall — which has its own stark, magnificent appeal.
